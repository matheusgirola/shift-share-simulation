# Testing Shift-Share Variants on an Artificial Dataset with Known Parameters

Companion code to the literature review *"Shift-Share Methodology in Regional
Economics: Historical Development, Variants, and Validity."* The goal is to
generate an artificial economy (**27 states × 5 sectors × 10 years**) whose
national, industry-mix and regional/competitive effects are known **by
construction**, and then test which shift-share variant best recovers them.

## How the artificial data solves the "which parameters?" problem

The key design decision is to place the true effects in **growth-rate space**,
exactly where every SSA variant assumes they live:

```
g[r,i,t] = n[t] + m[i,t] + c[r,i] + eps[r,i,t]
```

- `n[t]` — national rate each year (the National effect);
- `m[i,t]` — industry deviation, mean-zero each year (the Industry-Mix effect);
- `c[r,i] = c_bar[r] + d[r,i]` — a common regional advantage plus a
  region-industry interaction (the Competitive effect);
- `eps` — pure noise no model can recover.

Employment evolves multiplicatively year by year, so the additive identity
`ΔE = national + mix + competitive + noise` holds **exactly** in employment
units along the realized path (asserted in code to machine precision). That
gives every model an unambiguous ground truth to be scored against.

Two extra design features test specific claims from the literature:

1. **Allocation structure (Esteban-Marquillas test).** A third of the states
   start with industry shares tilted *toward* the industries where they hold
   an advantage (`well_allocated`), a third tilted *away*
   (`mis_allocated`), a third neutral. The true allocation effect is computed
   with path-wise homothetic employment, so the E-M allocation effect has a
   real target.
2. **Stress scenario (Barff-Knight test).** A second Monte Carlo raises the
   volatility of the national cycle, the yearly industry swings, and the
   noise — the exact conditions under which the literature says static
   endpoint models break down.

## Spatial extension (Nazara-Hewings test)

The 27 states carry the **actual land-border contiguity of the Brazilian
federation** (`brazil_geo.py`; row-standardized W). With `rho_space > 0`
the common regional advantage is generated by a SAR process,
`c_bar = (I − ρW)⁻¹u`, so a state's competitive rate is correlated with its
neighbors' (with ρ = 0.5, the true rates show Moran's I ≈ 0.66, p = 0.001).

The **Nazara-Hewings (2004)** model replaces the single national benchmark
in the competitive term with the neighborhood growth rate
`g̃[r,i] = Σₖ W[r,k]·g[k,i]`, splitting the classic competitive effect into a
**neighborhood effect** (is my neighborhood outgrowing the nation?) and an
**own-net effect** (am I outgrowing my own neighborhood?). Because the DGP
records the same split exactly (`true_neigh` uses W·c along the path), the
spatial decomposition has an unambiguous target. Both a static and a
Barff-Knight-style dynamic version are implemented.

**Spatial findings:**

- The NH split recovers both pieces well (neighborhood r ≈ 0.96, own-net
  r ≈ 0.98–1.00 over 50 seeds), with the dynamic version again beating the
  static one on RMSE; its *total* competitive effect is identical to classic
  Dunn's, as additivity requires.
- **Moran's I must be computed on competitive *rates*, not employment-unit
  levels.** Levels are dominated by state size and show no significant
  spatial pattern (I ≈ 0.12, n.s.) even when the true rates are strongly
  clustered; dividing by employment-years recovers I ≈ 0.63, p = 0.001 for
  every model. This is a practical warning for applied work.
- In the `no_space` (ρ = 0) scenario the NH machinery still measures the
  neighborhood component correctly — neighbors' rates exist even when
  independent — but that component carries no information about a state's
  own advantage and the true rates show no spatial clustering. NH is only
  *worth the extra structure* when spatial dependence is actually present,
  echoing the weight-matrix-sensitivity caveat in the review.

## Information-theoretic extension (Knudsen 2000)

Knudsen (2000) casts shift-share as a minimum-discrimination-information
(MDI) problem: minimize the Kullback-Leibler information gain of the terminal
employment distribution relative to the base one, subject to sector and
region totals. The Lagrangian solution is log-linear,
`ln(E1/E0) = A + O_i + D_r` (static, his eq. 18) or
`ln(E[t+1]/E[t]) = A + O_i + D_r + T_t` (dynamic, eq. 19), and the MDI
estimates are maximum-likelihood ones, so `knudsen_mdi` fits a Poisson GLM
with log link and offset `ln(E0)`. The regional effect `D_r` is re-centred to
an employment-weighted mean of zero and converted to employment units as
`(exp(D_r) − 1) · w_r`, as in the full-analogue regression.

Employment is not a Poisson count: the estimated dispersion is about 5 for the
dynamic form and over 400 for the static one (seed 42), and plain ML standard
errors change with the unit of measure (96% of states significant in jobs,
0% in thousands of jobs). `knudsen_mdi` therefore uses quasi-Poisson
(Pearson χ²/df) standard errors by default; `scale=None` gives the plain ML
errors of the paper. Point estimates are the same either way.

**Findings:** the dynamic form tracks the full-analogue regression almost
exactly (competitive rates correlate at 0.99999; Monte Carlo nRMSE 0.186
benign / 0.247 stress, against 0.182 / 0.245). The static form, a single
ten-year transition, lands behind classic Dunn with base weights on average
(0.243 / 0.295 against 0.212 / 0.289).

## Scoring

For each model, the estimated per-region **competitive effect** (the
component the whole literature fights about) is compared to the truth with
Pearson/Spearman correlation, normalized RMSE, and sign accuracy. Models in
the homothetic family (E-M, Arcelus I) are scored against the *homothetic*
truth, since that is the object they claim to estimate. Arcelus II is not in
that family: it partitions Dunn's competitive effect into a regional and a
differential shift, so its competitive column is scored against the ordinary
truth, and the two shifts against their own. The inferential
models (Berzeg ANOVA, full-analogue regression, Knudsen MDI) additionally get a power /
false-flag diagnostic on their significance tests.

## Headline results (50-seed Monte Carlo)

0. **Both Arcelus models degenerate in the regional aggregate, in different
   places.** Model I's differential-national and expected-mix terms are
   identically zero once summed over sectors; Model II's differential shift is
   identically minus the industry-mix effect, so it recovers nothing of the
   true within-region interaction (Pearson -0.20, nRMSE 1.16, sign accuracy
   44% -- worse than guessing the mean), while its regional shift tracks the
   true region-wide advantage at 0.84. Model II's competitive total is
   identical to classic Dunn with base weights, to machine precision.
1. **All variants rank regions almost identically** (Pearson ≈ 0.97–0.99) —
   consistent with Knudsen & Barff (1991): conventional and stochastic
   shift-share yield the same point conclusions.
2. **Magnitudes differ, and the gap widens under stress.** By normalized
   RMSE the ordering is: dynamic E-M ≈ full-analogue regression ≈ dynamic
   Barff-Knight ≈ Berzeg ≈ dynamic Knudsen < classic (base weights) <
   E-M/Arcelus I static < static Knudsen < Fuchs average weights < terminal
   weights. Under the volatile scenario the
   terminal-weight variant's error roughly doubles relative to the dynamic
   models — echoing Ashby's (1970) criticism of Stilwell and the
   Herzog-Olsen (1977) weight-sensitivity warning.
3. **The E-M allocation effect works as advertised**: estimated allocation
   effects are strongly positive for `well_allocated` states, strongly
   negative for `mis_allocated`, near zero for `neutral` — close to the true
   values.
4. **Only the inferential models can say whether an effect is real.** Berzeg
   ANOVA, the full-analogue regression and dynamic Knudsen flag 100% of the
   strongest-third regions as significant (seed 42); the deterministic variants produce numbers with
   no way to distinguish signal from noise — the core argument of Berzeg
   (1978) and Knudsen (2000).
5. Berzeg ANOVA and dynamic Barff-Knight produce **identical point
   estimates** (as theory predicts — Berzeg's model is the stochastic
   counterpart of the annual identity); the ANOVA form simply adds standard
   errors. This replicates Knudsen-Barff (1991) exactly.

## The real-data counterpart: RAIS 2022-2025

The simulation can score the variants because it knows the answer. To see what
they do when nobody does, the same thirteen variants run on formal employment
in the 27 states by CNAE 2.0 section, from the RAIS establishment microdata
(`ftp://ftp.mtps.gov.br/pdet/microdados/RAIS/<year>/RAIS_ESTAB_PUB.7z`,
active links on 31 December): 44.1 million jobs in 2022 to 48.7 million in
2025, +10.5%. Two layouts ship under the same filename — semicolon-separated
with a 5-digit CNAE class up to 2022, quoted comma-separated with a 7-digit
subclass from 2023 — and `rais_data._dialect` detects which.

With no truth to score against, the questions become how far the variants
disagree with each other and what the answer depends on. Headline results
(`run_rais.py`):

1. **They rank the states almost identically and disagree on magnitude** —
   the same split as the simulation, but wider: Pearson 0.72-1.00 against the
   Barff-Knight reference, while terminal weights sit 1.37 reference standard
   deviations away and move a state up to 15 places out of 27.
2. **In three states the sign is the analyst's choice.** São Paulo's
   competitive effect runs from −23.6k (full-analogue regression) to +212.6k
   (terminal weights) around a median of +13.5k; AM and AL also flip.
3. **A series break beats every methodological refinement.** From base year
   2023 the whole RAIS is collected through eSocial, and the re-labelling of
   public employment that came with it makes section O (administração
   pública) grow +176% in DF and −9% in SP. Including it shifts the
   competitive effect by nRMSE 3.8-4.0 — about four times the worst weighting
   choice and ten times the aggregation effect — and drops the rank
   correlation to 0.33-0.44. Section O is therefore out of the default panel
   (`rais_data.DROP_DEFAULT`, which documents the screen); shift-share is an
   identity and cannot tell a coverage break from a competitive gain.
4. **Aggregation bias is real, not theoretical** (Houston 1967): collapsing
   18 CNAE sections into 5 broad sectors moves the competitive effect by
   nRMSE 0.38-0.43.
5. **Almost nothing is significant.** Of 27 states, the full-analogue
   regression flags none at 5%, dynamic Knudsen 2, static Knudsen 3 and
   Berzeg 4 — against 100% power on the strong-advantage states in the
   simulation. The deterministic variants produce 27 precise-looking numbers
   per component with no way to know that most of them are indistinguishable
   from zero.
6. **No spatial clustering.** Moran's I of the estimated competitive rates is
   0.06-0.19 with p between 0.18 and 0.68 — none significant at 10%. This is
   the `no_space` scenario occurring in the wild: the Nazara-Hewings split
   stays correct (its total competitive effect equals Dunn's) but its
   neighborhood component carries no information about a state's own
   advantage, so the extra structure does not pay for itself at this
   geographic scale.

Caveats, stated in full in section 13 of the notebook: the counts come from
the establishment file rather than the links file and so run about 1% below
the ministry's published totals; sections P (education) and Q (health) carry
the same eSocial break as section O, smaller but not absent, and are kept
because their private employment is too large to discard.

## Interpreting / extending

- To make the test *harder*, raise `sd_eps` or shorten the panel; the
  aggregation-sensitivity test of Houston (1967) now runs on real data, via
  `rais_data.load_panel(level="grande")`.
- Natural next spatial experiments: alternative W specifications (k-nearest,
  inverse distance) to probe the Nazara-Hewings weight-matrix sensitivity;
  spatially autocorrelated *industry-specific* advantages (SAR on `d_ri`);
  or a spatial-econometric estimation step (Le Gallo-Kamarianakis 2011).
- The truth here is *time-constant* competitive rates. Real competitive
  effects are unstable over time (Brown 1969; Stevens-Moore 1980), so
  recovery in this sandbox is an upper bound on real-world performance —
  none of this validates SSA as a forecasting or causal tool.
- Natural next steps on the real panel: a longer window that does not
  straddle the eSocial migration; a municipal cut, where spatial dependence
  is more likely to exist than it does across 27 states; and the same
  decomposition on wages rather than headcount.
